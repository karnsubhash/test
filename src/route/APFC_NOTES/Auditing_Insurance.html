AUDITING

Examination of financial records to express an opinion on their truth & fairness

1Meaning, Nature & Objectives

The word audit comes from the Latin “audire” — meaning to hear. In ancient times, accounts were read out aloud and the auditor literally heard them to check for errors and fraud.

Definition Auditing is the systematic and independent examination of books of accounts, vouchers, records and financial statements of an organisation to ascertain whether they show a true and fair view of the financial position and results, and whether they comply with legal requirements. (Classic definition — Spicer & Pegler, and the modern ICAI view.)

Essential features (nature) of an audit

  • Systematic examination — carried out in a planned, orderly manner using an audit programme.
  • Independent & unbiased — the auditor must be free from influence of management.
  • Examination of evidence — based on vouchers, documents and other audit evidence.
  • Opinion, not certification of accuracy — the auditor gives an expert opinion on truth and fairness, he does not guarantee 100% accuracy.
  • Done by a qualified person — an independent professional (Chartered Accountant in India).

Objectives of audit

Primary objective Secondary (subsidiary) objectives
To express an opinion on whether the financial statements give a true and fair view and comply with the applicable financial reporting framework. (a) Detection of errors — errors of omission, commission, principle, and compensating errors.
(b) Detection of fraud — misappropriation of cash/goods, and manipulation of accounts (window dressing / secret reserves).
(c) Prevention — the moral/deterrent effect of a periodic audit discourages errors & fraud.
Mnemonic — 4 Errors “O-C-P-C” → Omission, Commission, Principle, Compensating.
Compensating error = two or more errors that cancel each other out → trial balance still tallies (hardest to detect).

2Advantages & Limitations of Auditing

Advantages

  • Reliability — audited accounts are trusted by shareholders, banks, tax authorities and investors.
  • Detection & prevention of errors and fraud; moral check on staff.
  • Legal recognition — accepted by courts, Income-Tax Dept., and regulators as evidence.
  • Aid to management — throws light on weaknesses of internal control; helps decision-making.
  • Settlement of accounts — useful for admission/retirement of partners, insurance claims, mergers, and valuation of business/goodwill.
  • Access to credit — lenders rely on audited statements.

Limitations

  • Not a guarantee of accuracy — audit gives reasonable assurance, not absolute assurance.
  • Test checking / sampling — the auditor examines only samples, so some errors may go undetected.
  • Dependence on explanations and information given by management — can be misled.
  • Inherent limitations of internal control and possibility of collusion / management override.
  • Judgement based — involves the auditor's personal judgement; conclusive proof is rarely available.
  • Costly & time-bound — cannot check every transaction within limited time.
Key point Audit provides “reasonable assurance”, NOT “absolute assurance”. This single line is a favourite one-mark trap.

3Types / Classification of Audit

A. On the basis of organisation / legal requirement

Type Meaning
Statutory audit Compulsory under a law (e.g. companies under the Companies Act 2013, banks, insurance companies). Auditor's appointment, rights & duties are fixed by statute.
Private / voluntary audit Not required by law but done voluntarily by sole traders, partnerships, individuals. Scope fixed by agreement.
Government audit Audit of government departments and PSUs, conducted by the CAG (Comptroller & Auditor General of India).
Internal audit Conducted by employees/independent agency appointed by management as an ongoing control tool.

B. On the basis of time / approach

Type Meaning & suitability
Continuous audit Audit continues throughout the year at regular intervals. Suited to large businesses with many transactions. Merit: early detection; Demerit: costly, staff may alter figures already checked.
Periodical / Final / Annual audit Begins after the close of the financial year and is completed in one continuous session. Suited to small & medium concerns.
Interim audit Conducted between two annual audits, usually to declare an interim dividend.
Balance-sheet audit Works backward from the balance sheet items to the entries — verification-focused. Common in the USA.

C. On the basis of scope / function

Complete audit (whole year checked), Partial audit (only a portion, e.g. only cash), Cost audit, Management audit, Tax audit, Cash audit, Special audit (covered in Topic 10).

Mnemonic — statutory bodies Company → CA, Government/PSU → CAG, Cooperative societies → Registrar/Govt auditor.

4Accounting vs Auditing vs Investigation

Basis Accounting Auditing Investigation
Meaning Recording, classifying & summarising transactions Independent examination of accounts to form an opinion Critical enquiry into accounts for a special purpose
Objective Ascertain profit/loss & financial position Verify truth & fairness of accounts Establish a specific fact (e.g. fraud, purchase of business)
Timing Continuous, before audit After accounting is complete Any time, as required
Scope Governed by accounting principles Governed by law & auditing standards Governed by terms of engagement
Done by Accountant Qualified independent auditor Auditor or expert (need not be a CA)
Nature of report — Opinion (true & fair) Detailed, purpose-specific
One-liner to remember “Accounting is the making of accounts; Auditing is the checking of accounts; Investigation is the searching of accounts for a special purpose.” Auditing begins where accounting ends.

5Internal Control, Internal Check & Internal Audit

These three are closely related and frequently confused — the distinction is a classic exam question.

Definitions Internal Control — the whole system of controls (financial and otherwise) established by management to conduct business in an orderly, efficient manner, safeguard assets and ensure reliable records. It is the umbrella concept.

Internal Check — an arrangement of duties such that no single person handles a transaction from beginning to end; the work of one person is automatically checked by another. It is built into the routine and needs no separate staff. (Part of internal control.)

Internal Audit — an independent appraisal activity within the organisation, done by separate staff/agency to review operations and controls as a service to management.
Basis Internal Check Internal Audit
Nature Part of the routine / arrangement of work Separate, independent review of the routine
Timing Runs simultaneously with the transaction Done after the transaction is recorded
Staff No separate staff — automatic Separate staff / agency required
Cost Economical Comparatively costly
Mnemonic — objectives of internal control “SEAR” → Safeguard assets · ensure Efficiency · ensure Accuracy & reliability of records · promote Reliability/adherence to policies (compliance).

Relationship: Internal Control is the widest term; Internal Check and Internal Audit are both parts of the internal control system.

6Vouching, Verification & Valuation

Vouching

Definition Vouching is the examination of documentary evidence (vouchers) to establish the authenticity, accuracy and validity of transactions recorded in the books. It is called the “backbone / essence of auditing.”

A voucher may be a receipt, invoice, bill, agreement, counterfoil, minute book, etc. While vouching the auditor checks: date, amount, account head, authorisation, and whether it relates to the business and the period under audit.

Verification & Valuation of assets and liabilities

Verification Valuation
Confirming existence, ownership, possession, and freedom from charge of assets/liabilities as on the balance-sheet date. Confirming that assets/liabilities are stated at their correct/appropriate value (as per accounting principles).
Wider term — includes valuation. Part of verification; primarily the responsibility of management, but the auditor must check its correctness.
Mnemonic — Verification checks “E-V-O-P” → Existence · Valuation · Ownership · Presentation (proper disclosure).
Exam trap Vouching ≠ Verification. Vouching relates to transactions in the books/entries; Verification relates to assets and liabilities shown in the balance sheet. “Vouching is the basis of verification.”

7Audit Programme, Note Book, Working Papers & Evidence

Tool What it is
Audit Programme A written plan listing the exact audit procedures, allotting work to audit staff with time schedules. Ensures nothing is left unchecked and fixes responsibility. Can be fixed or flexible.
Audit Note Book A register kept by audit staff to record queries, errors, doubtful points, explanations sought and points to be discussed with seniors/management. Useful evidence in case of negligence suits.
Audit Working Papers Documents prepared/obtained by the auditor supporting the audit — schedules, analyses, confirmations. They are the property of the auditor and support the audit opinion.
Audit Evidence Information used by the auditor to arrive at conclusions — documentary, physical, oral, and analytical. Should be sufficient & appropriate.
Key point Working papers are the property of the auditor (not the client). Audit evidence must be both sufficient (quantity) and appropriate (quality = relevant + reliable).

8The Auditor — Qualification, Appointment, Rights, Duties & Liabilities

(Company auditor under the Companies Act, 2013 — the most examinable framework.)

Qualification & appointment

  • Only a Chartered Accountant (or a firm where majority partners are CAs) can be appointed as a company auditor — Sec. 141.
  • First auditor appointed by the Board within 30 days of incorporation.
  • Subsequent auditors appointed by members at the AGM — Sec. 139 — normally for a term of 5 years, subject to ratification norms and rotation of auditors for prescribed companies.

Rights (powers) of an auditor — Sec. 143

  • Right of access at all times to books, accounts and vouchers.
  • Right to obtain information & explanations from officers.
  • Right to receive notice of and attend general meetings and be heard.
  • Right to sign the audit report and to remuneration.

Duties

  • To make a report to the members on the accounts and whether they show a true & fair view.
  • To enquire into loans, transactions, and to comply with Standards on Auditing.
  • To report fraud (above a threshold) to the Central Government — a duty added by the 2013 Act.

Liabilities

Civil liability — for negligence and breach of duty (damages). Criminal liability — for wilful false statements. Liability under the Companies Act and to third parties in certain cases.

Landmark case (interview-worthy) Kingston Cotton Mills Co. (1896): "An auditor is a watchdog, not a bloodhound" — he is justified in relying on trusted officials' representations unless there is suspicion. This defines the standard of reasonable care.

9Audit Report & Standards on Auditing

Audit report

The audit report is the end product of an audit — the auditor's written opinion. Types of opinion:

Opinion When given
Unqualified / Clean Accounts give a true & fair view — no reservations.
Qualified Generally true & fair except for certain matters (specific reservations).
Adverse / Negative Accounts do not give a true & fair view.
Disclaimer of opinion Auditor is unable to form an opinion (e.g. records not available / scope limited).

Standards on Auditing (SAs)

Issued in India by the ICAI (Institute of Chartered Accountants of India) through its Auditing & Assurance Standards Board. They lay down the principles and procedures every auditor must follow, giving audits uniformity and quality. Globally these correspond to the International Standards on Auditing (ISA) issued by IFAC.

Mnemonic — 4 opinions "U-Q-A-D" → Unqualified (best) → Qualified → Adverse → Disclaimer (worst / no opinion).

10Special Audits + the APFC / EPFO Angle

Audit Focus
Cost audit Verification of cost records & cost accounts; conducted by a Cost Accountant; ordered under the Companies Act for specified industries.
Management audit Appraisal of the efficiency of management and its functions — future-oriented, not just financial.
Tax audit Under Sec. 44AB, Income-Tax Act 1961 — audit of accounts of businesses/professionals crossing a turnover limit, done by a CA.
Social audit Evaluation of an organisation's social & ethical performance (e.g. MGNREGA social audits).
Performance / Propriety audit Done by CAG for government spending — checks economy, efficiency, effectiveness (3 E's) and "wisdom, faithfulness & economy".

Concepts like internal check, vouching and verification are exactly what an inspecting officer applies in the field.

INSURANCE

A contract that transfers the risk of a loss from one party to another for a premium

1Meaning, Nature & Functions of Insurance

Definition Insurance is a contract (policy) in which an individual/entity (the insured) receives financial protection or reimbursement against losses from an insurance company (the insurer), in return for a payment called the premium. In essence it is a co-operative device to spread the loss of a few over many exposed to the same risk.

Nature / characteristics

  • A contract — governed by the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and the Insurance Act, 1938.
  • Risk-sharing & risk-transfer device — pools risk among many.
  • Co-operative device — losses of the unfortunate few are met from the common fund of premiums.
  • Based on the law of large numbers & probability — the more the members, the more accurate the prediction of loss.
  • Payment made on a contingency — only if the insured event happens (except life, which is a certainty of timing).
  • Not gambling & not charity — the insured has an insurable interest; premium is scientifically calculated.

Functions of insurance

Primary Secondary Other
Provide certainty (of loss payment)
Provide protection
Risk-sharing 		Prevention of loss
Provides capital / mobilises savings
Improves efficiency
Helps economic progress 		Means of savings & investment
Promotes foreign trade
Source of employment
Mnemonic — insurance is NOT… Insurance is not gambling (insured must suffer a real loss) and not charity (premium is charged). Wager = create risk; Insurance = transfer existing risk.

2Principles of Insurance

The 7 fundamental principles — the single most important, most-asked area in the whole topic.

# Principle Meaning
1 Utmost Good Faith (Uberrimae Fidei) Both parties must disclose all material facts truthfully. Non-disclosure/misrepresentation makes the contract voidable. Applies to all insurance.
2 Insurable Interest The insured must have a financial/legal stake in the subject-matter — he benefits from its safety and suffers from its loss. Without it the contract is void (it becomes a wager).
3 Indemnity The insured is placed in the same financial position as before the loss — no profit from insurance. Applies to general insurance (fire, marine, motor), NOT to life.
4 Subrogation After paying the claim, the insurer steps into the shoes of the insured and takes over his rights against a third party. A corollary of indemnity.
5 Contribution Where the same risk is insured with more than one insurer, each insurer shares the loss rateably; the insured cannot recover more than the actual loss. Corollary of indemnity.
6 Proximate Cause (Causa Proxima) The nearest / effective cause of loss (not remote cause) decides whether the insurer is liable.
7 Loss Minimisation The insured must take reasonable steps to minimise the loss, as any prudent uninsured person would.
Mnemonic — 7 principles "U-I-I-S-C-P-L" → Utmost good faith · Insurable interest · Indemnity · Subrogation · Contribution · Proximate cause · Loss minimisation.
High-yield distinctions • Indemnity, Subrogation & Contribution DO NOT apply to Life insurance (life can't be valued in money). They apply to general insurance.
• Utmost good faith & Insurable interest apply to ALL insurance.
• In life insurance, insurable interest must exist at the time of taking the policy; in fire, at the time of policy AND loss; in marine, at the time of loss.

3Types of Insurance

Broadly two categories: Life insurance and General (Non-life) insurance.

Life insurance

Covers the risk of death and provides savings/investment. Main plans: Term (pure risk cover), Whole-life, Endowment, Money-back, ULIP (Unit-Linked), and Annuity/Pension plans.

General (non-life) insurance

Type Covers
Fire insurance Loss/damage to property by fire & allied perils.
Marine insurance Loss to ship (hull), cargo & freight during sea transit. (Oldest form of insurance.)
Motor insurance Vehicles — third-party cover is compulsory under the Motor Vehicles Act.
Health / Medical insurance Hospitalisation & medical expenses (e.g. Mediclaim, Ayushman Bharat).
Crop / Agriculture Loss of crops (e.g. PM Fasal Bima Yojana).
Others Burglary, fidelity guarantee, liability, travel, personal accident, etc.
Key facts Marine insurance is the oldest form of insurance. Motor third-party liability is legally compulsory. Life = protection + savings; General = pure protection / indemnity.

4Life vs General Insurance

Basis Life Insurance General Insurance
Subject-matter Human life Property, goods, liability, health
Nature of event Certain (death is certain, only timing uncertain) Uncertain / contingent (may or may not happen)
Principle of indemnity Does NOT apply (life has no money value) Applies
Insurable interest Needed at time of taking the policy Needed at time of loss (fire: both times)
Duration Long-term Usually short-term (often 1 year, renewable)
Element Protection + savings/investment Only protection
Claim Sum assured paid in full on death/maturity Actual loss reimbursed (up to sum insured)

5Reinsurance & Double Insurance

Basis Reinsurance Double Insurance
Meaning An insurer insures part of its own risk with another insurer (a re-insurer) to reduce its burden. The same risk is insured with two or more insurers by the insured.
Who does it The insurer The insured
Purpose Spread very large risks Extra safety / full recovery
Claim Insured deals only with the original insurer Insured cannot recover more than actual loss (principle of contribution applies)

India's national reinsurer: GIC Re (General Insurance Corporation of India).

6Role of Insurance in Economic Development

  • Mobilises savings — premiums collected form a huge pool of long-term funds.
  • Capital formation & investment — insurers invest in government securities, infrastructure and industry.
  • Provides security — encourages entrepreneurship & risk-taking.
  • Promotes trade & commerce — marine & credit insurance enable exports/imports.
  • Reduces the burden on the State — private provision of social security.
  • Generates employment and supports financial-market development.
Mnemonic "SCS-TER" → Savings mobilisation · Capital formation · Security · Trade promotion · Employment · Reduces state burden.

7Insurance in India — History & IRDAI

Timeline of key milestones

Year Milestone
1818 Oriental Life Insurance Company — first life insurer in India (Kolkata).
1938 Insurance Act, 1938 — first comprehensive law to regulate insurance (still the parent Act).
1956 Nationalisation of life insurance → LIC formed (LIC Act, 1956).
1972 Nationalisation of general insurance → GIC and its 4 subsidiaries (General Insurance Business Nationalisation Act, 1972).
1993 Malhotra Committee recommended opening up the sector to private players.
1999 IRDA Act, 1999 passed → regulator established (began functioning 2000); sector opened to private & foreign participation.
2014 Renamed IRDAI; FDI raised from 26% → 49%.
2015 Insurance Laws (Amendment) Act, 2015.
2021 FDI in insurance raised from 49% → 74%.
2025 Union Budget 2025-26 proposed raising FDI to 100% (for insurers investing the entire premium in India) via the Insurance Amendment Bill — enabling legislation/rules were under process (to be notified).
IRDAI — the regulator Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India. A statutory body under the IRDA Act, 1999, headquartered at Hyderabad. Functions: protect policyholders, licence & regulate insurers/agents, promote & ensure orderly growth of the sector, and fix conduct/solvency norms. Vision: "Insurance for All by 2047."
Mnemonic — LIC & GIC years Life → 56, General → 72. (Life comes first alphabetically and chronologically.) Regulator IRDA → 99.

8Insurance & Social Security (the APFC core link)

For APFC, insurance is not just commercial — it underpins social security. Insurance-based government schemes are directly relevant:

Scheme Nature
EDLI (Employees' Deposit-Linked Insurance), 1976 Life-insurance cover for EPF members — a lump sum to nominees on death of the member in service. Administered by EPFO — core to APFC work.
PMJJBY (Jeevan Jyoti Bima Yojana) Government life-insurance cover (renewable term) for bank-account holders.
PMSBY (Suraksha Bima Yojana) Accident-insurance cover at a very low premium.
Ayushman Bharat (PM-JAY) Health-insurance cover for poor & vulnerable families.
Atal Pension Yojana / ESIC Pension & medical-benefit social-security schemes.
Why the examiner tests this APFC works under the EPF & MP Act, 1952 administering three funds — EPF, EPS (Pension), and EDLI (Insurance). EDLI is a pure insurance scheme, so the principles of insurance (insurable interest, contract, risk-pooling) apply directly. Expect linkage questions between insurance ↔ social security ↔ EPFO.
